A Letter About Pals

I’m not sure how I feel about social media anymore.

Top 8 friends, texting 140-character updates to 40404, a chronological feed of your friends’ vacation photos — there was a time when being online with friends felt smaller and safer. But at some point, something shifted.

I stopped seeing my friends’ updates and started seeing content instead. Strangers commented on things posted for a select few. The feed stopped being chronological and started being algorithmic — because outrage and envy keep people scrolling longer than a friend’s Tuesday afternoon ever could. And the companies behind it all profited from every extra minute.

Personally, I miss early Instagram — that feeling of sharing intentionally. Texting apps are fine, but they’re designed for making plans, not sharing a life. And I know I’m not alone in feeling this way, so I began to design something new. I call it Pals, and if you’ve spent a few minutes tapping through the demo, you might already understand how Pals is different.

To join Pals, you need an invite from a person you know, and after a short onboarding process, you already have your first pal in the app. That real-world connection removes “stranger-danger” and allows people to be authentic, not performative.

Pals is designed to be entirely private. Messaging, connections, and user profiles are all end-to-end encrypted. Pals doesn’t generate a social graph or suggest “pals you may know” because the network is designed to prevent that kind of creepy tracking.

This also means your data can’t be sold, and the app has no ads. Pals is delightfully quiet: no influencers, no bots, just friends.

You might be asking, without ads, how does Pals generate income? The idea is Pals should generate income through donors and digital in-app purchases, like subscriptions and digital items. The Pals parent company will be a non-profit, which eliminates pressure from shareholders to increase profit margins and caps executive salaries at reasonable levels. It may also have an added effect of restoring some trust from a public feeling trapped by corporate greed.

We’re at an inflection point for social media. Countries around the world are beginning to enforce changes to the status quo. Australia led the way with a nationwide ban for people 16 and under. Here in the US, Attorneys General in almost every state reached an $18 billion settlement with Meta to enforce time limits and reduce algorithmic addictions for kids and teens, but it feels like a Band-Aid to a larger societal problem. Instead of retrofitting a broken system, let’s build something better from the ground up.

Pals exists today only as a demo on the web, and that’s because designing privacy right is a real challenge. The parts that are easy to fake — the look and feel — are easy to build. But real end-to-end encryption and a server that genuinely can’t read your messages are difficult problems to solve, and getting them wrong would be worse than not building them at all.

Pals has a lot of challenges, starting with the fact that it’s another app in an already crowded space. But I believe Pals is doing something uniquely different: when someone signs up for Pals, they’re no longer the product, they’re the customer. I think this focus is why Pals can succeed, but we’ll need more than a vision and a functional demo. Pals needs patient, altruistic funding to invest in a better digital future and overcome the challenges of a startup competing in an entrenched digital space.

But I think this is the time to challenge that entrenchment. I think people are ready for real, honest change, Pals was built with the belief that social media can be safe and fun. Together, we can make the internet a little better.

Thanks for reading.
-Brandon
